      Privacy, in plain words

      • No GPS by default. The page only offers to use your device location through a small prompt, and nothing is requested from the browser until you press “Allow location” (or “Show me on the map”).
      • Approximate only. Your browser asks a public IP-lookup service (ipwho.is, or geojs.io as a backup) for a rough place based on your network. That service sees your IP address, as any website would. We use only the district name from the answer and throw the rest away — your IP address and coordinates are never stored or shown.
      • What is saved: a running total for each district, a count of unique visitors, a count of total page visits (every page load adds one), and a private record under a random anonymous Firebase ID saying when this browser first visited, when it last loaded the page, and which district it is counted in. Only your own browser can read that record. If (and only if) you publish a pin, one blurred point is also stored under the same random ID, together with a display name only if you choose to type one. No emails, phone numbers or profiles exist anywhere in this system.
      • District totals only. A district appears on the map and in the list as soon as 1 person is counted there. Only the district-level total is shown, never who is counted.
      • “Show me on the map” is optional. Your browser asks permission to use GPS. Your exact coordinates never leave your device: the page shifts them by a random 500 m–1 km, rounds them to about 100 m (the database rejects anything finer), tells you which district the pin lands near, and publishes only after you confirm. Everyone else sees a marker; if you added a name, they see it when they tap your marker, otherwise it stays anonymous. Please use a nickname you are comfortable showing publicly. The same random shift is reused each time, so repeated sharing can't be averaged to find you.
      • You stay in control of the map pin. If you choose to show a pin, a “Hide me” button lets you take it off the map at any time. Sharing again moves your pin. Your district count itself is anonymous and automatic, with nothing personal attached.
      • Limits. IP locations can be wrong (mobile data, VPNs, university networks). Clearing browser data or using another browser can lead to being counted again. A blurred pin still shows roughly where you are (within about a kilometre), so skip it if that is sensitive.

      Student-run, unofficial project. Map boundaries: geoBoundaries (CC BY 4.0), simplified for the web, so borders are approximate. Public pins, if anyone chooses to share one, are blurred by 0.5–1 km.
